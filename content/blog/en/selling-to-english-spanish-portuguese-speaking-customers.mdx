In 2021, 7.1% of all US employer firms were Hispanic-owned, a national baseline that climbs sharply in key states. Florida alone had 85,966 Hispanic-owned businesses representing 18.1% of the state's total employer firms ([U.S. Census Bureau, 2024](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2024/10/hispanic-owned-businesses.html), accessed Sep. 2026). California ranked first by count with 88,920 firms, meaning the states where service businesses most often ask us about multilingual marketing (Florida, California, and Texas) all sit in the top tier nationally for Hispanic business concentration.

## How the Bilingual Customer Actually Searches

A bilingual customer might search for a plumber in English in the morning and then message friends on WhatsApp about it in Spanish that afternoon. The friend shares a link they found through a Spanish Google search. Missing any of those touchpoints means your business never appears in the decision process, and you never know the call was lost.

Bilingual digital behavior follows context, not a fixed language preference. In 2024, Pew Research Center found that 54% of US Latinos consume news mostly in English, 21% mostly in Spanish, and 23% about equally in both ([Pew Research Center, "US Hispanics' consumption of English- and Spanish-language news", Mar. 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2024/03/19/english-and-spanish-language-news-consumption-among-hispanics/), accessed Sep. 2026). That split reflects how the same audience uses different languages in different contexts: English for formal and professional queries, Spanish for community services and personal recommendations.

This context-switching matters most in local service categories. Home services, cleaning, healthcare, and food feel like community referrals, not corporate transactions. When your business shows up in Spanish for those categories, it signals cultural belonging, not just linguistic match. A Spanish-speaking customer who finds a cleaning company or contractor through a Spanish-language search is far more likely to share that business within their community, on WhatsApp, in Facebook groups, and in person, than if they had found it through a generic English search. That referral loop is the channel that English-only campaigns can't access at any budget level.

The practical implication: marketing in Spanish isn't just about reaching more people with the same message. It's about entering a trust network that operates in Spanish and shares service recommendations in Spanish. A competitor who shows up first in that channel doesn't just win the individual click. They get embedded in the recommendation layer that drives word-of-mouth calls for months afterward.

## Spanish-Language Campaigns Are Not Just Translated Ads

Translating your English ad headline into Spanish isn't a Spanish campaign. The search queries are different: "limpieza de casas cerca de mi" competes against different advertisers than "house cleaning near me," often at lower cost per click. Native Spanish copy also performs better because it resonates culturally, not just linguistically.

Spanish-language ad groups in local service categories, including cleaning, HVAC, and construction, often face less competition than equivalent English ad groups. Fewer local businesses tend to bid on Spanish keywords. A smaller advertiser with a well-built Spanish campaign can reach that audience at a lower cost per lead than in English, not because clicks are inherently cheap, but because the competitive field has not filled up yet. Check it in your own account instead of assuming it.

Before we launch any Spanish campaign for a client, two questions always come first. Does the business have someone who can answer a call in Spanish? Running Spanish ads to an English-only phone line wastes the click and damages the impression a new caller has of your business. The second question is: which neighborhoods? Not every zip code in a city has the same language mix. Starting with that map prevents budget waste and puts the investment where the audience actually is.

Spanish keyword research is a separate exercise from English, not a translation task. Spanish speakers use different query patterns, often including neighborhood-level references or community context that English queries skip. Ad extensions, call assets, and landing pages must match the language end to end. "Hablamos español" in a call extension tells callers before they click that someone will understand them, and that single line improves both click-through and conversion rates for Spanish-speaking audiences.

A bilingual setup done right isn't twice the work. The keyword research, ad copy, and landing pages for Spanish are separate assets, but the management cadence is the same: review performance weekly, adjust bids monthly, test new ad variations quarterly. The only meaningful additional cost is the initial setup and the call-answering capacity in Spanish. ## The Portuguese-Speaking Audience Most Agencies Forget

Portuguese-speaking customers in the US concentrate in specific cities: Greater Boston, Newark, and parts of Houston. They aren't reached by Spanish campaigns, since the languages are entirely distinct and a Spanish ad doesn't rank for Portuguese queries. In those markets, a Portuguese-language presence competes against almost nobody, because almost nobody builds one.

In Massachusetts, Brazilian entrepreneurs own approximately 24,488 medium-sized and growing companies, and Brazilian immigrant workers contributed around $8 billion to the state's gross domestic product in 2022 ([Instituto Diáspora Brasil / The Boston Foundation, 2024, via GBH News](https://www.wgbh.org/news/local/2024-10-24/new-report-says-brazilians-the-biggest-immigrant-group-in-mass-shouldnt-be-forgotten), accessed Sep. 2026). The same community that generates that economic output also produces a dense consumer base in Framingham, Somerville, and the Newark corridor. Those communities search for services in Portuguese, ask for recommendations in Portuguese, and trust service providers who can communicate in Portuguese.

Portuguese-language queries for local services look nothing like Spanish equivalents. "Encanador perto de mim" is a different query from "plomero cerca de mi," competing against a different set of zero advertisers. For a plumber, cleaner, or contractor in Newark, adding a Portuguese-language Google Business Profile description and one Portuguese-language service page costs almost nothing extra if the business already has bilingual infrastructure. The barrier isn't budget. It's knowing the audience exists at all. Most US marketing agencies don't speak Portuguese and don't build Portuguese-language campaigns. That creates a structural gap: a business willing to publish Portuguese-language content competes against almost zero other local businesses in that search channel, in a city where the community is large, economically active, and ignored by the marketing industry.

## Multilingual Paid Ads Without Doubling the Budget

A bilingual Google Ads campaign uses one shared daily budget split across language-segmented ad groups. English and Spanish audiences peak at different times, search with different query patterns, and respond to different calls to action. One campaign structure, two audiences: total reach increases while the budget stays the same.

A bilingual structure with a shared budget can lower overall cost per lead compared with concentrating entirely on English. Spanish ad groups can fill hours when English competition peaks and cost per click spikes. The shared budget shifts toward the ad group with the most active search volume at that time of day. Total spend stays constant, but total leads can increase because more of the budget flows to clicks that cost less.

So which structure should you choose? A single campaign with language-segmented ad groups works well at lower daily budgets: the shared cap prevents overspending in one language while the other lags. At higher spend, separate campaigns give more control over daily budgets, service areas, and call routing, especially if the business has separate phone lines for English and Spanish inquiries. That separation also makes performance data cleaner: you can see exactly what each language audience costs per lead and optimize them independently.

The practical checklist before launching bilingual paid ads: separate keyword lists for each language (not auto-translated), a language-specific landing page or at minimum a language-specific call to action, call assets set up in the language of the ad, and at least one team member who can take the first call in that language. Each of these is a setup decision, not a recurring cost. The ongoing management looks like any monolingual campaign: test copy, adjust bids, refine match types. The setup is where most businesses either get it right or lose the investment on day one. ## City Snapshots: Houston, Newark and Miami

Houston is home to one of the largest Spanish-speaking urban populations in the US. Newark has dense Brazilian, Portuguese, and Central American communities within a few square miles. Miami is Spanish-dominant in most practical business contexts. In all three, running English-only local marketing isn't a conservative choice: it's a competitive disadvantage that a bilingual competitor will exploit as soon as they notice the gap.

Hispanic-owned employer firms concentrate in a handful of states: California leads the nation with 88,920, Florida follows with 85,966, and Texas is home to one of the largest Hispanic populations in the country ([U.S. Census Bureau, 2024](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2024/10/hispanic-owned-businesses.html), accessed Sep. 2026). Specific Houston neighborhoods are majority Spanish-speaking in daily use, meaning that for a service business operating in those zip codes, an English-only ad campaign reaches the minority of the local customer base. The Spanish-language majority is available to any competitor willing to build a bilingual campaign. In most Houston service categories, that competitor hasn't appeared yet.

Newark sits at the center of a Portuguese-Spanish corridor extending from the Brazilian communities around Framingham and Somerville in Massachusetts down to the Dominican and Central American neighborhoods of New Jersey. Brazilian entrepreneurs own approximately 24,488 businesses in Massachusetts alone ([Instituto Diáspora Brasil / The Boston Foundation, 2024, via GBH News](https://www.wgbh.org/news/local/2024-10-24/new-report-says-brazilians-the-biggest-immigrant-group-in-mass-shouldnt-be-forgotten), accessed Sep. 2026), and the community extends directly into Newark and the surrounding area. A service business in Newark with Portuguese and Spanish language presence has almost no competition in those search channels.

Miami is the most Spanish-mature of the three. In most Miami service neighborhoods, Spanish is the dominant everyday language. Running English-only ads in many Miami zip codes is already the underdog play. The question there isn't whether to market in Spanish: it's which Spanish-speaking communities and cultural registers your services speak to most naturally.

The Boston-Newark corridor for Portuguese speakers is the most structurally underserved opportunity across the Etuos markets. Houston and Miami have maturing Spanish ad competition. A business willing to publish Portuguese-language content and run one Portuguese-language ad group builds local search authority with almost zero competitive pressure and a community that's large, economically active, and has been ignored by the marketing industry for years.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a separate website for Spanish-speaking customers?

No. A separate website isn't required and adds more maintenance overhead than benefit for most local service businesses starting out. A translated service page, a bilingual GBP description, and Spanish ad groups pointing to a language-aware landing page are enough to start. A fully separate site is only justified at high spend volume with dedicated teams managing each language version.

### How do I know if my city has enough Spanish speakers to make bilingual marketing worth it?

Start with your own inquiry data: how many of your current calls or messages come in Spanish without any effort to attract them? If a meaningful share of inbound contacts already comes in Spanish, a bilingual campaign can grow that share. The cost of a test is low. The cost of ignoring it is a permanent share of your market going to whoever showed up in that language first.

### Should I hire a Spanish-speaking team member before running Spanish ads?

Yes, ideally. Running Spanish ads to an English-only phone team wastes the click and damages the first impression. At minimum, have one person who can take an initial call and schedule an appointment in Spanish. Your ad can reference this directly: "Hablamos español" in a call extension tells callers before they click that someone will understand them.

### Can I just translate my English ads into Spanish?

Translation is a starting point, not a strategy. Spanish queries differ from English queries in structure, vocabulary, and intent. A translated ad running against an English-derived keyword list misses most of the actual Spanish search volume because the searches your Spanish-speaking audience types are often completely different from translated English queries. Start with native Spanish keyword research, then write ad copy that speaks to that audience's specific motivations.
