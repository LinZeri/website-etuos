That gap isn't an edge case. In cities like Houston, Newark, and Miami, many potential customers search for local services in Spanish or Portuguese. Most US service businesses build their marketing as if one language covers everyone. It doesn't. This guide covers how to reach English, Spanish, and Portuguese-speaking customers in the same city, without running three separate businesses or tripling your marketing budget.

In cities like Houston, Newark, and Miami, the multilingual customer isn't a niche segment: they're part of the local market that English-only campaigns leave uncontested.

In 2021, 7.1% of all US employer firms were Hispanic-owned, a national baseline that climbs sharply in key states. Florida alone had 85,966 Hispanic-owned businesses representing 18.1% of the state's total employer firms ([U.S. Census Bureau, 2024](https://www.census.gov/library/stories/2024/10/hispanic-owned-businesses.html), accessed Sep. 2026). Florida, California, and Texas all sit well above the national average in Hispanic-owned firm counts.

## How the Bilingual Customer Actually Searches

A bilingual customer might search for a plumber in English in the morning and then message friends on WhatsApp about it in Spanish that afternoon. The friend shares a link they found through a Spanish Google search. If you don't show up at one of those touchpoints, your business may never enter the decision process, and you never know the call was lost.

Bilingual customers don't have a single fixed language. In 2024, Pew Research Center found that among US Hispanic adults, 54% get news mostly in English, 21% mostly in Spanish, and 23% about equally in both ([Pew Research Center, "US Hispanics' consumption of English- and Spanish-language news", Mar. 2024](https://www.pewresearch.org/journalism/2024/03/19/english-and-spanish-language-news-consumption-among-hispanics/), accessed Sep. 2026). That is news consumption, not search behavior, but it shows that a large share of this audience uses both languages. Treat it as a reason to test Spanish for your services, not as proof of how people search.

Language choice may matter most in local service categories. Home services, cleaning, healthcare, and food often run on community referrals. When your business shows up in Spanish for those categories, it signals that you understand the community, not just the language. A customer who finds you in Spanish can more easily recommend you in Spanish, on WhatsApp, in Facebook groups, and in person. An English-only presence has a harder time entering that referral loop.

The practical implication: marketing in Spanish isn't just about reaching more people with the same message. It's about entering a trust network that shares service recommendations in Spanish. A competitor who shows up first in that channel doesn't just win the individual click: they can become the name people recommend.

## Spanish-Language Campaigns Are Not Just Translated Ads

![Illustration of two differently designed ad cards connected by a green arrow, showing adaptation instead of translation](/images/blog/selling-to-english-spanish-portuguese-speaking-customers-2-ads.webp)

Translating your English ad headline into Spanish isn't a Spanish campaign. The search queries are different: "limpieza de casas cerca de mi" competes against a different set of advertisers than "house cleaning near me." Native Spanish copy also speaks to the customer in the words they actually use, not just in their language.

In some local service categories, Spanish-language ad groups may face less competition than the English equivalents, because fewer local businesses build Spanish campaigns. If that holds in your market, a well-built Spanish campaign can reach that audience at a lower cost per lead. It depends on your city and category, so check it in your own account instead of assuming it.

Before you launch any Spanish campaign, two questions come first. Does the business have someone who can answer a call in Spanish? Running Spanish ads to an English-only phone line wastes the click and damages the impression a new caller has of your business. The second question is: which neighborhoods? Not every zip code in a city has the same language mix. Starting with that map prevents budget waste and puts the investment where the audience actually is.

Spanish keyword research is a separate exercise from English, not a translation task. Spanish speakers use different query patterns, often including neighborhood-level references or community context that English queries skip. Ad extensions, call assets, and landing pages must match the language end to end. "Hablamos español" in a call extension tells callers before they click that someone will understand them, and that single line sets the right expectation before the call.

A bilingual setup done right isn't twice the work. The keyword research, ad copy, and landing pages for Spanish are separate assets, but the management routine is the same: review performance, adjust bids, test new ad variations. The only meaningful additional cost is the initial setup and the call-answering capacity in Spanish. ## The Portuguese-Speaking Audience Most Agencies Forget

![Illustration of a map pin above a small group of people with a green magnifier circling them](/images/blog/selling-to-english-spanish-portuguese-speaking-customers-3-community.webp)

Portuguese-speaking customers in the US concentrate in specific cities: Greater Boston, Newark, and parts of Houston. They aren't reached by Spanish campaigns: Portuguese and Spanish are different languages, and a Spanish ad is not built around Portuguese queries. In those markets, a Portuguese-language presence is likely to face far fewer competitors, because few businesses build one.

In Massachusetts, Brazilian entrepreneurs own approximately 24,488 medium-sized and growing companies, and Brazilian immigrant workers contributed around $8 billion to the gross state product in 2022 ([Instituto Diáspora Brasil / The Boston Foundation, 2024, via GBH News](https://www.wgbh.org/news/local/2024-10-24/new-report-says-brazilians-the-biggest-immigrant-group-in-mass-shouldnt-be-forgotten), accessed Sep. 2026). A community with that economic weight is also a consumer base, and many of its members are likely to prefer a provider who can communicate in Portuguese.

Portuguese-language queries for local services look nothing like Spanish equivalents. "Encanador perto de mim" is a different query from "plomero cerca de mi," which is a different search from the Spanish one. For a plumber, cleaner, or contractor in Newark, adding a Portuguese-language Google Business Profile description and one Portuguese-language service page costs almost nothing extra if the business already has bilingual infrastructure. The barrier isn't budget. It's knowing the audience exists at all. Few marketing providers build Portuguese-language campaigns, so a business willing to publish Portuguese-language content may face little competition in that search channel.

## Multilingual Paid Ads Without Doubling the Budget

A bilingual Google Ads campaign can use one shared daily budget split across language-segmented ad groups. English and Spanish audiences search with different query patterns and respond to different calls to action. One campaign structure, two audiences: you reach more people without raising the budget.

A bilingual structure with a shared budget may lower your overall cost per lead compared with concentrating entirely on English, if the Spanish ad groups turn out cheaper in your market. Total spend stays constant, and the budget flows toward the ad groups that produce leads. Your own account data decides whether that happens.

So which structure should you choose? A single campaign with language-segmented ad groups works well at lower daily budgets: the shared cap prevents overspending in one language while the other lags. At higher spend, separate campaigns give more control over daily budgets, service areas, and call routing, especially if the business has separate phone lines for English and Spanish inquiries. That separation also makes performance data cleaner: you can see exactly what each language audience costs per lead and optimize them independently.

The practical checklist before launching bilingual paid ads: separate keyword lists for each language (not auto-translated), a language-specific landing page or at minimum a language-specific call to action, call assets set up in the language of the ad, and at least one team member who can take the first call in that language. Each of these is a setup decision, not a recurring cost. The ongoing management looks like any monolingual campaign: test copy, adjust bids, refine match types. The setup is where most businesses either get it right or lose the investment on day one. ## Frequently Asked Questions

### Do I need a separate website for Spanish-speaking customers?

No. A separate website isn't required and adds more maintenance overhead than benefit for most local service businesses starting out. A translated service page, a bilingual GBP description, and Spanish ad groups pointing to a language-aware landing page are enough to start. A fully separate site is only justified at high spend volume with dedicated teams managing each language version.

### How do I know if my city has enough Spanish speakers to make bilingual marketing worth it?

Start with your own inquiry data: how many of your current calls or messages come in Spanish without any effort to attract them? If a meaningful share of inbound contacts already comes in Spanish, a bilingual campaign can grow that share. A small test is a low-risk way to find out. Ignoring it can leave that share of the market to whoever shows up in that language first.

### Should I hire a Spanish-speaking team member before running Spanish ads?

Yes, ideally. Running Spanish ads to an English-only phone team wastes the click and damages the first impression. At minimum, have one person who can take an initial call and schedule an appointment in Spanish. Your ad can reference this directly: "Hablamos español" in a call extension tells callers before they click that someone will understand them.

### Can I just translate my English ads into Spanish?

Translation is a starting point, not a strategy. Spanish queries differ from English queries in structure, vocabulary, and intent. A translated ad running against an English-derived keyword list can miss much of the actual Spanish search volume, because the searches your Spanish-speaking audience types are often different from translated English queries. Start with native Spanish keyword research, then write ad copy that speaks to that audience's specific motivations.
