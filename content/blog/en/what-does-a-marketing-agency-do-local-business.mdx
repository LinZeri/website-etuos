A marketing agency does four jobs for a local business: it helps people find you in local search, it runs paid ads, it builds or fixes the pages those people land on, and it measures what happened. Everything else on an agency's menu is a variation or a label.

That sounds simple, and the jobs are. What confuses owners is the gap between what an agency can control (the setup, the work, the reporting) and what it cannot (rankings, rules written by Google, whether your phone gets answered). This guide separates the two, so you can read any proposal, ours included, against the same standard.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - An agency's work falls into four jobs: local search, paid ads, website and landing pages, and measurement. If a service does not fit one of them, ask what it is for.
> - No agency controls Google. Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking, and it does not guarantee that it will crawl, index or serve a page. Anyone who promises a position is promising something outside their control.
> - A good report answers three questions: what was done, what changed, and what happens next. Activity lists without outcomes do not count.
> - The work only pays off if you keep your side: answer the phone, serve customers well and own your accounts.
> - Disclosure: Etuos is a marketing agency, so we have an interest here. Apply every test in this guide to us too.

## What Is a Marketing Agency, in Practical Terms?

An agency is a team you hire to do marketing work you cannot or do not want to do yourself, on an ongoing basis. For a local service business, that usually means more people finding you and calling, not "brand awareness."

The label hides large differences. Some agencies specialize in one channel, such as paid search or local SEO. Others sell a bundle. Some hand you a strategy and leave the work to you. Others do the work and hand you a report. When you compare agencies, compare the jobs they do, not the titles on the proposal.

## The Four Jobs an Agency Does for a Local Business

The rest of this guide takes each job in turn: what the work is, what a competent version looks like and what the agency cannot do. ### Job 1: Local Search (Getting Found on Google and Maps)

This is the work of making your business easier to find when someone nearby searches for what you do. In practice it covers your Google Business Profile, the accuracy of your business information, reviews and the content on your website.

Google describes what drives local results: they are mainly based on relevance, distance and popularity, and the page adds that how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have feed into the third factor ([Google, "Improve your local ranking on Google", Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091), accessed Oct. 2026). That tells you what a local search agency can influence. Relevance and prominence can be worked on. Distance cannot. If a competitor sits closer to the searcher, no agency can move your office.

Here is what the work should include:

- **A profile that follows Google's rules.** Google says a rented mailing address where the business does not operate, known as a virtual office, is not eligible for a Business Profile, and that P.O. boxes or mailboxes at remote locations are not acceptable ([Google, "Guidelines for representing your business on Google", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177), accessed Oct. 2026). The same page tells a service-area business to hide its address from customers. An agency that "fixes" your visibility with a made-up address is creating a suspension risk, not an advantage.
- **Reviews asked for the right way.** Google's content policy forbids offering incentives such as payment, discounts or free goods or services in exchange for a review, and forbids selectively soliciting positive reviews ([Google, Business Profile Help, "Prohibited & restricted content"](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7400114), accessed Oct. 2026). The help page on asking for reviews says customers can be asked with a Google link or a QR code, and that they must be signed into a Google Account to leave one ([Google, Business Profile Help, "Ask for Google reviews"](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474122), accessed Oct. 2026). Asking every customer, with no filter, is the compliant method.
- **Website pages built for the services and areas you actually serve.** Not copies of one page with the city name swapped.

**What the agency cannot do:** buy you a better local position. Google's own page says "There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google." If an agency sells that, it is selling something that does not exist. For the full picture of how the work fits together, read our [local SEO guide for service businesses](/en/blog/local-seo-for-service-businesses-complete-guide), and for the profile itself, the [Google Business Profile optimization checklist](/en/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist).

### Job 2: Paid Ads (Paying to Be in Front of People Now)

Paid ads put your business in front of people who are searching or scrolling, and you pay for the attention. For local service businesses this mostly means Google Ads, Local Services Ads and ads on social platforms.

An agency's job here is to set up, run and improve the account. A competent one can explain how the auction works, because that explains why costs move. Google's page on Ad Rank says that when you set a bid you are telling Google Ads the maximum you are willing to pay for a click, and that how much you actually end up paying is often less. The same page lists what goes into Ad Rank: your bid, the quality of your ads and landing page, the Ad Rank thresholds, the competitiveness of an auction, the context of the person's search and the expected impact from your ad assets and other ad formats ([Google, "About Ad Rank", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722122), accessed Oct. 2026).

![A gavel leaning on a laptop with a green dial on its blank screen, next to a jar of coins](/images/blog/what-does-a-marketing-agency-do-local-business-2-ads.webp)

Three facts help you judge the work:

1. **A bigger bid is not the whole story.** Because Ad Rank mixes bid with ad and landing page quality, an agency that only raises bids is using one lever of several.
2. **Quality Score is a diagnostic, not a lever.** Google says Quality Score is not an input in the ad auction. It is measured on a scale from 1 to 10 and built from expected click-through rate, ad relevance and landing page experience ([Google, "About Quality Score for Search campaigns", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6167118), accessed Oct. 2026). An agency that promises to "raise your Quality Score" as a goal in itself is misreading its own tool.
3. **Cost estimates are estimates.** Average cost per click is the total cost of your clicks divided by the number of clicks ([Google, "Average cost-per-click (Avg. CPC): Definition", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/14074), accessed Oct. 2026). The top of page bid estimate is the bid you likely need for your ad to show among the first results, and Google states that it is not a guarantee ([Google, "Top of page bid estimate: Definition", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6292661), accessed Oct. 2026). Keyword Planner, Google's research tool, shows the average cost for your ad to show on searches for a keyword and forecasts clicks, conversions or impressions from a spend ([Google, "Use Keyword Planner", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7337243), accessed Oct. 2026). A good agency will use these tools in front of you and call a forecast what it is.

Local Services Ads work on a different billing model. Google says lead prices may vary by location, job type, type of lead or bidding mode, and that leads determined to be invalid or low quality are not charged ([Google, "How leads work", Local Services Help](https://support.google.com/localservices/answer/7195435), accessed Oct. 2026). If your agency manages LSA, ask how it checks the leads you are charged for. Our guide to the [Google Verified badge on Local Services Ads](/en/blog/google-guaranteed-badge-local-services-ads) covers that channel, and [how much paid ads cost a local business](/en/blog/how-much-paid-ads-cost-local-business-guide) covers budgeting.

**What the agency cannot do:** make people call. It can buy the click and build the page. Whether the visitor becomes a customer also depends on your offer, your price and how fast you answer.

### Job 3: The Website and Landing Pages (Where Visitors Decide)

Ads and local search send people somewhere. If that place is slow, confusing or hard to use on a phone, the earlier work is wasted. So agencies often build or rework your pages: the home page, one page per service, pages for the areas you serve and a simple way to call or message you.

Competent work here is specific. Each page has one clear job. The phone number or message button is easy to reach. The content matches what the ad or search promised. Google's own advice for content starts with a question: does it provide original information, reporting, research or analysis ([Google Search Central, "Creating helpful, reliable, people-first content"](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/creating-helpful-content), accessed Oct. 2026)? That is a good test for any page an agency delivers.

**What the agency cannot do:** guarantee that a published page appears on Google. Google says it does not guarantee that it will crawl, index or serve your page, and that Googlebot does not crawl all the pages it discovered ([Google Search Central, "In-depth guide to how Google Search works"](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/how-search-works), accessed Oct. 2026). A new site is a starting line. For what a site's design and build involve, see our [website design service](/en/services/website-design).

### Job 4: Measurement and Reporting (Proving What Changed)

The fourth job is the one owners skip when they compare agencies, and it decides whether you can ever tell if the other three worked. An agency should set up tracking so that calls, messages and form submissions can be tied back to where they came from, and then report on it.

For search, the reference tool is Google Search Console. Google says it helps you understand and improve how Google sees your site ([Google, "About Search Console", Search Console Help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9128668), accessed Oct. 2026). Its performance report defines the basic numbers: clicks are the number of times a user clicked your site from Google Search results, impressions are how many times your site appeared in Search results, and click-through rate is the click count divided by the impression count ([Google, "Performance report (Search results)", Search Console Help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7576553), accessed Oct. 2026). If an agency's report shows search numbers, they should line up with these definitions. Our [Search Console guide for local business owners](/en/blog/google-search-console-for-local-business-owners) shows how to read them yourself.

**What the agency cannot do:** make a number mean something it does not. Impressions are not customers. A report full of impressions with no calls attached is a presentation, not a result.

## What No Agency Can Sell You

Some promises sit outside any agency's control. Treat them as a signal about the agency, not as a feature.

![A sealed envelope with a green seal next to a closed padlock and a blank sheet of paper](/images/blog/what-does-a-marketing-agency-do-local-business-3-limits.webp)

- **A guaranteed position.** Google's page on local ranking says there is no way to request or pay for a better one, and its page on how Search works says it does not guarantee that it will crawl, index or serve a page.
- **A guaranteed number of leads or sales.** Leads depend on demand in your area, your competitors, your offer and your follow-up. The top of page bid estimate itself comes with Google's own line that it is not a guarantee.
- **A shortcut around Google's rules.** Fake addresses, bought reviews and reviews pulled only from happy customers are exactly what the policies above prohibit. Google says violations can lead to actions that range from suspending account privileges to account termination.

A trustworthy agency tells you what it controls and what it does not, and writes the first list in the contract and the second in the plan.

## What Stays With You

An agency does not replace the parts of the business only you can run. Three things stay on your side of the table.

1. **Answering.** A click that reaches a voicemail is a lost customer. For Local Services Ads, Google says that if you regularly fail to answer calls or respond to messages, your ad ranking may be affected ([Google, "Getting started with Local Services Ads", Local Services Help](https://support.google.com/localservices/answer/6224841), accessed Oct. 2026). What happens to a lead after it arrives is its own subject, covered in [lead management for a small business](/en/blog/lead-management-for-small-business) and [what to do with leads from Google Ads](/en/blog/what-to-do-with-leads-from-google-ads).
2. **The real work.** Good marketing makes a customer try you once. Your service decides whether the reviews that follow are worth having.
3. **Ownership of your accounts.** Your Business Profile, your Search Console property and your ad account should be yours, with the agency added as a user. Google says a verified owner has the highest degree of permissions in Search Console ([Google, "Verify your site ownership", Search Console Help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9008080), accessed Oct. 2026). Whoever holds that role controls access. Ownership gets its own guide on this blog, so we will not stretch this section.

## What a Good Report Looks Like

Reports are where an agency's habits show. A useful one is short and tells you what to decide. Use this table to read any report, including ours.

![A printed report on a clipboard made of simple blocks, one line highlighted in green, with a pen beside it](/images/blog/what-does-a-marketing-agency-do-local-business-4-report.webp)

| Report area | What it should show | A weak version looks like |
|---|---|---|
| Work done | What was changed, added or fixed, in plain language | A list of tasks with no reason attached |
| Local search | Profile changes, review activity, questions or calls from the profile, in Google's own terms | A "visibility score" you cannot check |
| Paid ads | Spend, the leads it produced, which searches triggered the ads, what was changed | Impressions and clicks with no calls or messages |
| Website | Which pages bring calls or messages and which do not | Traffic totals with no link to contacts |
| Next steps | One to three decisions, with a reason for each | "Continue current strategy" |

Two more tests. First, can you check the numbers yourself? You should be able to open your own ad account, Business Profile and Search Console and see the same data. Second, does the report ever say something unwelcome? An agency that has never told you a channel is not working is either lucky or not looking.

## Do You Need an Agency at All?

Not always. Hiring one makes sense when three conditions hold: the work is more than you can do well yourself, your phone and calendar can handle more customers, and you are willing to look at the reports. If your calendar is already full, more marketing is not your problem. If nobody answers the phone, fix that first.

A freelancer, a part-time hire or doing the basics yourself can be the right call for a small budget or a narrow need. Our guides on the [first marketing dollars to spend](/en/blog/where-to-spend-first-marketing-dollars-local-business) and on [why a business does not show on Google Maps](/en/blog/why-my-business-not-showing-google-maps) cover what to fix before paying anyone.

An agency earns its place when the work needs several skills at once (search, ads, pages, tracking) and you want one team accountable for how they fit.

## Red Flags You Can Spot Before You Sign

You do not need to be technical to catch these.

- **A promise of position, leads or timing.** The sources above explain why none of it is in anyone's control.
- **A plan that includes a fake or borrowed address.** Google's guidelines rule out virtual offices and remote P.O. boxes.
- **Review tactics that screen or reward.** Rewards and selective asking are on Google's prohibited list.
- **No access for you.** If you cannot see your own accounts, you cannot check anything.
- **Vague answers about what is actually done each month.** The work should be nameable.
- **A business phone number the agency controls.** Google's guidelines say the phone number must be under the direct control of the business.

## Checks You Can Run Yourself

You can verify more than you think.

1. **Search the agency's own business.** Do they show up for what they sell, in their own market? An agency that cannot market itself is a weak sign.
2. **Look up their advertising.** Google keeps a searchable repository of advertisers and the ads they have served, called the Ads Transparency Center, and you can search it by advertiser or website name ([Google, "Ads transparency", Advertising Policies Help](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/13733850), accessed Oct. 2026). The version of that page we could open is the Austria edition, so treat it as a general description, not a statement about the US page. Use it to see what an agency's ads actually say.
3. **Ask to see the accounts, not a screenshot.** A live view of your own ad account beats any slide.
4. **Ask for the review request method in writing.** It should match Google's rules.

These checks are the start of choosing between agencies, not the whole job. A full checklist comes next on this blog.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What does a marketing agency do for a small business?

An agency does four jobs: local search work such as your Google Business Profile and reviews, paid ads, building or fixing the website and landing pages, and measuring results. A good one reports on what it did and what changed.

### Can a marketing agency guarantee first place on Google?

No. Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking, and it does not guarantee that it will crawl, index or serve a page. Treat any promise of a position as a warning sign.

### Do I need a marketing agency for my local business?

Not always. An agency helps when the work needs several skills at once and you can handle more customers. If your phone goes unanswered or your calendar is full, fix that first.

### What should a marketing agency report show?

It should show what was done, what changed and what happens next. Calls, messages and form submissions matter more than impressions, and you should be able to see the same data in your own accounts.

### Should I own my Google accounts if an agency runs them?

Yes. Your Business Profile, Search Console property and ad account should be in your name, with the agency added as a user. A verified owner has the highest degree of permissions in Search Console.
