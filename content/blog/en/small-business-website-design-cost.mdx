Ask five web designers what a small business website costs and you will get five very different answers. That is not a trick. It is the honest result of a simple fact: "a website" can mean a one-page business card or a custom booking platform, and the price follows the scope.

This guide does not hand you a price table. We do not have a verified, nationwide source for one, and a made-up range would only give you a number to anchor on that has nothing to do with your business. Instead, you will see what actually moves the price, what you keep paying after launch, how to budget with your own numbers, and how to read a quote.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - There is no single correct price. Cost follows scope: pages, custom design, content, features, languages and who builds it.
> - A website has ongoing costs too: domain, hosting, updates and security. Ask about them before you sign.
> - Budget from the value of a customer, not from the cheapest quote. A site is worth what the calls and bookings it produces are worth to you.
> - Make sure you own your domain, your content and your accounts, whoever builds the site.

## Why Is There No Single Price for a Small Business Website?

![Illustration of three browser windows of different sizes stacked like building blocks, next to a pencil and a ruler](/images/blog/small-business-website-design-cost-1-scope.webp)

Two plumbers can both say "I need a website" and mean completely different things. One wants a clean page with a phone number, a list of services and a few photos. The other wants service pages for every city, online scheduling, a way to pay a deposit, and a version in Spanish.

Those are different jobs, done by different people, taking different amounts of work. The price is mostly the cost of that work, plus the cost of the skills behind it.

That is why a flat number you find online is rarely useful. It usually describes someone else's project. A better question than "what does a website cost?" is "what does my website need to do, and who is best placed to build that?"

## What Actually Drives the Cost of a Website?

![Illustration of an open grey toolbox holding a paintbrush, a green wrench, a camera and a shopping cart](/images/blog/small-business-website-design-cost-2-drivers.webp)

Almost every quote comes down to the same handful of drivers. When two quotes look far apart, one of these is usually the reason.

**1. Number and type of pages.** A site with a home page, a services page, an about page and a contact page is a smaller job than one with a separate page for each service and each city you serve. More pages mean more writing, more design decisions and more testing.

**2. Template or custom design.** A template starts from a ready-made layout that gets adjusted to your brand. A custom design is drawn for your business from scratch. Templates are generally the lighter job and custom work the heavier one, but a good template can serve a simple business well. Custom pays off when your brand or your process is genuinely unusual.

**3. Who writes the words and takes the photos.** This is the most underestimated line item. A site is only as persuasive as its copy and images. If the designer writes the text and arranges a photo shoot, that is real work and it shows up in the price. If you supply everything, the quote shrinks, but the quality of the result depends on what you hand over. Stock photos are cheap and look like stock photos.

**4. Features.** Each extra feature adds build time and something that can break: online booking, a quote calculator, payments, a customer login, live chat, a store. If a feature does not help a customer call, book or buy, question whether it belongs in the first version.

**5. Languages.** A site in English and Spanish, or in English and Portuguese, is not one site with a translation button. Each language needs its own pages, written for that audience. It costs more, and for many local businesses it is worth it: a global CSA Research survey found that 76% of online shoppers prefer to buy products with information in their native language ([CSA Research, 2020](https://csa-research.com/l/media/Consumers-Prefer-their-Own-Language), accessed Sep. 2026). That is a global figure about shopping preferences, not a measure of your city, but it shows why language is a business decision and not a cosmetic one.

**6. Search setup.** A site that nobody can find does not do its job. Clean page structure, fast loading, local pages and the right titles are part of building a site that can be found. Ask whether the quote includes this or treats it as a separate service. Our [complete guide to local SEO for service businesses](/en/blog/local-seo-for-service-businesses-complete-guide) explains what that work involves.

**7. Who builds it.** Doing it yourself with a website builder costs the least in cash and the most in your own hours. A freelancer is typically a leaner option with one person's skills. An agency brings a team (design, writing, development, strategy) and a process, and usually charges for that. None is automatically better. Match the builder to the size of the job and the amount of risk you can tolerate.

Here is the same list as a quick comparison. It is qualitative on purpose: it shows which direction each driver pushes the price, not how much.

| Driver | Usually a lighter job | Usually a heavier job |
|---|---|---|
| Pages | A few core pages | A page per service and per city |
| Design | Adjusted template | Custom design from scratch |
| Content | You supply text and photos | Builder writes copy and arranges photos |
| Features | Contact form and phone button | Booking, payments, customer login |
| Languages | One language | Separate pages for each audience |
| Search setup | Basic page titles | Local pages, speed work, structure |

## What Do You Keep Paying for After Launch?

![Illustration of a house-shaped website icon with a key, a padlock, a wrench and a green gear](/images/blog/small-business-website-design-cost-3-upkeep.webp)

The build is the headline number. The costs that follow are easier to forget, so list them before you decide.

- **Domain name.** Your web address is rented, usually with a renewal. Register it in your own name.
- **Hosting.** The service that keeps your site online. It is a recurring charge, and the type of hosting affects speed and reliability.
- **Maintenance and security.** Software needs updates, and sites that are not updated can become a security risk. Ask who handles this and whether it is included.
- **Content changes.** New services, new photos, a price change, a holiday notice. Ask whether small edits are included or billed each time.
- **Add-ons.** Paid plugins, booking software, email tools and similar subscriptions can quietly stack up.

When you compare two quotes, compare the full picture: the build, plus what you will pay to keep the site running and current. A cheap build with expensive upkeep can cost more than a pricier build that includes the basics.

## How Do You Decide What a Website Is Worth to You?

![Illustration of a balance scale with a stack of coins on one side and a smartphone with a green phone icon on the other](/images/blog/small-business-website-design-cost-4-budget.webp)

The most useful way to set a budget is to start from what a customer is worth to you, not from what other people paid. You already know your own numbers.

Here is a simple way to run the math. The figures below are **hypothetical and for illustration only**, not market data and not a price anyone should expect.

> *Illustrative example:* suppose a new website would cost $6,000 in total, and an average job earns you $500 in profit after your costs. Divide the first number by the second: $6,000 / $500 = 12. The site would pay for itself once it brings in 12 jobs you would not otherwise have won.

Now replace those made-up numbers with yours. Take your real profit per job, estimate how many extra jobs a better site could plausibly bring, and compare the result to the quote. If the math only works with a wildly optimistic guess, the budget is too high or the plan is too thin. If it works comfortably with a cautious guess, you have room to invest.

Two cautions. First, a website does not produce customers on its own. It converts the attention you get from Google, referrals, ads and your profile into calls. A beautiful site with no traffic has no effect. Second, never base a budget on a promise that a site will "rank number one" or pay for itself by a certain date. Nobody can guarantee that, and anyone who does is selling you confidence, not a result.

## How Do You Compare Quotes Without Getting Burned?

Put the quotes side by side and ask the same questions of each. A good designer will answer them without hesitation.

1. **What exactly is included?** Number of pages, who writes the text, who sources the photos, and what happens with your existing content.
2. **Who owns what?** The domain, hosting account, design files and content should be yours. If a builder keeps control of your domain, you can end up unable to leave.
3. **What does it cost to keep the site running?** Hosting, maintenance, and small changes after launch.
4. **How is it built?** Does it work well on a phone? Many customers will open your site on a phone, and a site that is hard to use on a small screen loses them.
5. **Is the search setup included?** Page titles, structure, speed and local pages should be part of the conversation, not an afterthought.
6. **What are the payment terms?** Be careful with arrangements that charge everything upfront, or that lock you into a long contract just to keep your site live.
7. **What happens if you want to leave?** You should be able to take your site and your content with you.

Be wary of the quote that is far cheaper than the rest without explaining why, and equally of the one that cannot explain what you are paying for. The point is not to find the lowest price. It is to understand what each price buys.

## Does a Website Need to Work With Ads and Google Profiles?

Yes, and this is where many small business sites quietly fail. If you run paid ads, the page people land on matters. Google's own documentation describes landing page experience as one of the three components of Quality Score, defined as "How relevant and useful your landing page is to people who click your ad" ([Google, "About Quality Score for Search campaigns", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6167118), accessed Oct. 2026). Google also notes that Quality Score is a diagnostic tool and not an input in the ad auction, so treat it as a way to spot problems, not as a lever. For how ad budgets work, see our guide to [what paid ads cost a local business](/en/blog/how-much-paid-ads-cost-local-business-guide).

A site also supports your Google Business Profile. Google's guidelines say not to give a phone number or URL that redirects people elsewhere ([Google, "Guidelines for representing your business on Google", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177), accessed Oct. 2026), so the website you list should be your own and should match your business information.

The takeaway: a website is the center of your local marketing, not a separate line item. Budget for it together with how customers will arrive.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How much does a small business website cost?

There is no single price. Cost depends on the number of pages, custom design versus a template, who writes the content, the features you need, the number of languages and who builds it. Compare quotes by what they include and by what you will pay after launch, not by the headline number alone.

### Why are website quotes so different from each other?

Because the scope behind each quote is usually different. One may include writing, photos and search setup, another may not. Ask each builder to list exactly what is included before comparing prices.

### What ongoing costs should I expect after launch?

Plan for your domain renewal, hosting, maintenance and security updates, small content changes and any paid tools or plugins. Ask each builder which of these are included and which are billed separately.

### Should I build my website myself or hire someone?

It depends on your time, your skills and how much the site matters to winning customers. A website builder costs less in cash and more in your own hours. Hiring a professional costs more in cash and saves you time and design risk.

### Do I own my website and domain?

You should. Register the domain in your own name and keep control of your hosting account, content and design files. Ask any builder to confirm this in writing.

### How do I know if a website is worth the money?

Start from what a customer is worth to you. Divide the total cost by your profit per job to see how many extra jobs the site needs to bring in to pay for itself, then judge whether that is realistic for your market.

### Does my website need to be in more than one language?

If part of your customer base searches and buys in Spanish or Portuguese, a version written for that audience can help. Each language needs its own pages, so it adds to the cost. Decide based on who your customers actually are.

## What Should You Do Next?

Write down what your website needs to do, in plain words: what a visitor should do, which pages they need and what you cannot do without. Work out what one new customer is worth to you. Then ask two or three builders to quote the same list, and compare what is included, what you own and what it costs to keep running.
