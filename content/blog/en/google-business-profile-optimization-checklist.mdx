Your Google Business Profile is one of the biggest factors in whether your business shows up on Google Maps. It carries more weight than most owners expect, and often more than your website does.

Google says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance, and prominence, and that you can't pay for a better local ranking ([Google, "Improve your local ranking on Google", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091), accessed Oct. 2026). Your profile is the part of that equation you control directly. And many service business owners claimed their profile years ago, filled out half the fields, and never went back.

An incomplete profile tells Google you exist but gives it little reason to rank you above the competitor down the street who keeps theirs complete and current. This checklist covers every section of your Google Business Profile in priority order, so you fix the highest-impact items first and stop guessing what matters.

## Should You Verify Your Profile Before Anything Else?

![Illustration of a storefront under a map pin with a verification postcard arriving and a green shield badge with a check mark](/images/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist-1-verify.webp)

Yes. Google says you need to verify your business to edit your business info, such as your name or hours, and to interact with customers ([Google, "Verify your business", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7107242), accessed Oct. 2026). Until you complete it, you cannot manage the profile.

The verification options available to you depend on your business type, region, and other details. Google determines the method automatically and you can't change it. Do not edit your business name, address, or category while you wait for a verification code. Once verified, you control how the profile shows and can respond to customers.

If you haven't verified yet, nothing else on this checklist matters. Start there. Everything else builds on a verified foundation.

## How Do You Choose the Right Primary Category?

Your primary category is one of the clearest relevance signals your profile sends. Google says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance, and prominence, and relevance is how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for ([Google, "Improve your local ranking on Google", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091), accessed Oct. 2026). Getting the category wrong costs you relevance on every search.

Google's guidelines tell businesses to choose categories that are as specific as possible but representative of their main business, and to use as few categories as possible ([Google, "Guidelines for representing your business on Google", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177), accessed Oct. 2026). A general contractor who primarily does HVAC work should list "HVAC Contractor," not "General Contractor."

Here's how to find the right one. Search your service plus your city on Google Maps and look at the top three listings. What category do they use? That's usually the most relevant match for your type of work.

A wrong primary category keeps a profile out of the searches it should win. An HVAC company listed as "General Contractor" describes itself too broadly, and a more specific primary category matches the profile to the work it actually does.

Secondary categories help too. A house cleaning company might set "House Cleaning Service" as primary and add "Carpet Cleaning Service" and "Move-In Cleaning Service" as secondary. But keep the primary category as the one that describes your core business.

## What Does a Complete Profile Look Like?

![Illustration of a business profile card with a storefront photo, phone, hours and location rows all completed with check marks](/images/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist-2-profile.webp)

In BrightLocal's 2025 survey, 83% of consumers said they use Google to read reviews of local businesses, ahead of local news sites (48%), Yelp (44%), Facebook (40%), and YouTube (34%) ([BrightLocal, "Local Consumer Review Survey 2025", 2025, via SearchLab Digital](https://searchlabdigital.com/blog/data-from-brightlocals-consumer-review-survey/), accessed Sep. 2026). When those consumers find your listing, every empty field is a missed opportunity. A complete Google Business Profile gives Google more signals to match your business to relevant local searches, and gives customers fewer reasons to look elsewhere.

Here is every field that matters, in order:

- **Business name.** Use the name your business has in the real world. Google says including unnecessary information in the name isn't permitted, and it can lead to suspension, so skip keywords and city names.
- **Address vs. service area.** If customers come to you (a storefront), show your address. If you go to them (a plumber, a cleaner, an electrician), hide the address and set your service area. Google's guidelines say a service-area business should hide its business address from customers. This is called a service-area business (SAB) setup.
- **Phone number.** Google says to use a local phone number instead of a central call center helpline number whenever possible, and the number must be under the direct control of the business.
- **Website URL.** Add UTM parameters so you can track clicks from your profile separately in Google Analytics. Something like `?utm_source=google&utm_medium=organic&utm_campaign=gbp`.
- **Hours.** Set your regular hours. Add holiday hours and special hours when they apply, and keep them accurate so customers know when to call.
- **Business description.** Lead with your primary service and city. Write for the customer, not for Google. Google's guidelines do not allow links, promotions, or prices in the description.
- **Attributes.** Mark what applies: woman-owned, veteran-owned, wheelchair accessible, online appointments. These show up in your listing and help customers filter results.

## Why Should You Add Photos Every Week?

Customers want to see the real business before they call. Photos are one of the easiest parts of your profile to improve, and there is no guarantee of a specific result, but real images answer questions a description can't.

For a service business, this means photographing completed jobs, your team at work, your vehicle, and your workspace regularly. One real photo per week builds a library of real work over time. Here's what to shoot:

- **Exterior shots.** Your storefront, office, or branded vehicle. This helps customers recognize you and confirms your location.
- **Team photos.** People trust people. Show your crew doing actual work, not posed stock-photo smiles.
- **Completed jobs.** Before-and-after photos are powerful for contractors, cleaners, and landscapers.
- **Interior.** Your office, showroom, or workspace. Clean, well-lit, honest.

Skip stock photos. Customers want to see your actual team and your actual work, and a real photo of a finished job is more honest than a polished stock image.

Upload at least one photo per week. You don't need a photographer. A phone in good light is enough. Over a year, that is 52 photos of real work.

## How Do You Build a Review Engine That Drives Ranking?

![Illustration of five stars in a row, the last one in green, beside a review speech bubble](/images/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist-3-reviews.webp)

Consumer expectations around star ratings are rising. In 2026, 31% of US consumers will only use a business rated 4.5 stars or higher, up from 17% in 2025, and 41% say they always read reviews, up from 29% ([BrightLocal, "Local Consumer Review Survey 2026", 2026, via PinMeTo](https://www.pinmeto.com/news/brightlocal-local-consumer-review-survey-2026/), accessed Oct. 2026; survey of 1,002 US adults, Feb. 2026).

Google also says more reviews and positive ratings can help your local ranking ([Google, "Improve your local ranking on Google", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091), accessed Oct. 2026). Reviews affect whether a customer calls you, and replying to them shows customers you are paying attention.

The review strategy that works for service businesses is simple:

1. **Ask right after the job.** Satisfaction is highest the moment you finish. Send the customer the review link by text or email, without pressure. Google's policy says merchants should not require or pressure users to leave reviews while on the premises ([Google, "Prohibited & restricted content", Maps User Generated Content Policy Help](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114), accessed Oct. 2026).
2. **Make it one tap.** Get your review link or QR code from your Business Profile and drop it into a text message. Google says the QR code can only be generated on a computer browser ([Google, "Create a Google link or QR code to request reviews", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/16816815), accessed Oct. 2026). Don't make customers hunt for your listing.
3. **Respond to every review.** Positive reviews get a short, personal thank you. Negative reviews get a calm, professional response that addresses the issue. In 2026, 80% of consumers say they are more likely to use a business that replies to all of its reviews ([BrightLocal, 2026, via PinMeTo](https://www.pinmeto.com/news/brightlocal-local-consumer-review-survey-2026/), accessed Oct. 2026). That is a stated preference, not a guaranteed result.
4. **Never offer incentives.** Google's policy lists offering incentives such as payment, discounts, or free goods or services for reviews as fake engagement that will be removed, and violations can lead to account suspension ([Google, "Prohibited & restricted content", Maps User Generated Content Policy Help](https://support.google.com/contributionpolicy/answer/7400114), accessed Oct. 2026).

Also, in the 2025 edition of the survey, 74% of consumers said they check at least two review sites before choosing a local business ([BrightLocal, "Local Consumer Review Survey 2025", 2025, via SearchLab Digital](https://searchlabdigital.com/blog/data-from-brightlocals-consumer-review-survey/), accessed Sep. 2026). Your Google reviews matter most, but don't ignore Yelp, Facebook, or industry-specific platforms.

Recency matters, not just total count. In 2026, 74% of consumers look for reviews written in the last three months ([BrightLocal, 2026, via PinMeTo](https://www.pinmeto.com/news/brightlocal-local-consumer-review-survey-2026/), accessed Oct. 2026). A steady flow of new reviews beats a pile of old ones.

## Are You Using Google Posts and Services?

In 2026, 47% of US adults reported using an AI tool for local search in the previous month, according to a Yext survey of 1,120 adults ([Yext, "2026 Consumer Search Behaviors Report", 2026](https://www.yext.com/resources/consumer-search-behaviors-findings), accessed Sep. 2026; Yext sells local presence management software). Google Posts and the Products and Services sections give your profile additional content for customers to read, including people who now ask AI tools about local businesses. Many competitors leave these sections empty.

Google Posts and the Products and Services sections are free tools many competitors ignore. Posting regularly shows customers your business is active. Here's how to use each one:

**Google Posts.** Publish a short update regularly. Include a photo and a call to action (call now, book online, learn more). Keep posts brief. Topics can be seasonal tips, completed projects, special offers, or team news.

**Common questions.** Put the questions your customers actually ask on the phone into your description, your services and your website: "Do you offer free estimates?" "What areas do you serve?" "Are you licensed and insured?" Write clear, direct answers. Google has been changing how questions and answers work on Maps, so check what your profile currently shows.

**Products and Services.** List every service you offer with a short description. This is especially valuable for [service businesses across the US](/en/usa) because it gives Google explicit signals about what you do, beyond your category alone.

## Can You Reach Customers Who Search in More Than One Language?

![Illustration of two speech bubbles facing a map pin with a green globe between them, representing customers searching in different languages](/images/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist-4-languages.webp)

Most guides to Google Business Profile optimization treat the profile as monolingual. In practice, a cleaning company in [Miami](/en/cities/miami) or a contractor in Newark with an English-only presence may be harder to find and trust for customers who search in Spanish or Portuguese. In markets like Miami, Houston, or Newark, some of your potential customers search in those languages. Here is what you can do:

- **Answer in the customer's language.** Reply to reviews in the language the customer wrote in, and make sure your team can handle calls and messages in Spanish or Portuguese if you advertise it. Don't repeat your business name in multiple languages: Google's guidelines don't allow it.
- **Match your website content.** If your profile links to a page in English but the customer searched in Spanish, you'll lose them. Consider building a [bilingual or multilingual website](/en/blog/selling-to-english-spanish-portuguese-speaking-customers) that matches what your customers expect.
- **Check how your profile reads.** Search for your service in Spanish or Portuguese and see whether your business, category and website show up in a way that makes sense to those customers.

In cities with large bilingual populations, this is a gap many competitors never address.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Is Google Business Profile free?

Yes. Claiming, verifying, and optimizing your Google Business Profile costs nothing. Google charges only if you choose to run ads through Google Ads. The profile itself, including Posts and reviews, is free for every business.

### How long does it take for changes to show up?

There is no fixed timeline you can count on. Verification options and review times depend on your business, and some changes take longer than others to show up in local results. Check your profile after each edit.

### Does my Google Business Profile affect my website's organic ranking?

Indirectly. GBP signals drive local pack rankings, which operate as a separate system from organic search results. A strong profile builds brand prominence online, which can help branded organic queries over time. But the direct impact is on the map pack, not on your website's position in the ten blue links.

### How do I get more reviews without being pushy?

Ask right after a completed job, when satisfaction is highest. Send a text with a direct review link, without pressure. The key is timing and ease: one tap, no hunting for your listing. Never offer discounts or gifts in exchange for reviews. Google's policies prohibit it.

### Can I rank in the map pack for cities where I don't have an office?

Yes, if you are a service-area business. Define your service area in your GBP settings and build a [unique landing page for each city](/en/services/seo) on your website. Proximity still matters: ranking in distant cities requires stronger prominence signals (reviews, links, authority) to overcome the distance disadvantage.

## What Should You Fix First?

Your Google Business Profile is not a set-it-and-forget-it asset. Verify it, pick the most specific primary category, complete every field, add photos every week, build reviews systematically, post regularly, and address the multilingual gap if your market calls for it.

The order matters. Verification first. Category second. Then completeness, photos, reviews, and ongoing activity. Each item on this checklist compounds the one before it.
