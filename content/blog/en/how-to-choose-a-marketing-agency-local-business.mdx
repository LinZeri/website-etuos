Most owners pick an agency the way they pick a restaurant in an unfamiliar city: a good-looking pitch, a friendly call, a quick decision. The pitch tells you very little about what happens after you sign.

This checklist gives you criteria you can test before you hire anyone. One disclosure first: Etuos is a marketing agency, so we have an interest in this topic. Apply every point below to us as well. If an agency cannot pass these checks, it should not pass them for you either.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - Decide what you need fixed before you talk to anyone. An agency that starts with a package instead of your problem is selling, not diagnosing.
> - Insist that your Google Business Profile, Google Ads account and Search Console are set up under your own ownership, not the agency's.
> - Treat any promise of a ranking position as a red flag. Google says there is no way to pay for a better local ranking and that it does not guarantee a page will be crawled, indexed or served.
> - Ask for a report you can read: clicks, impressions, click-through rate and average position are defined by Google, and your own call and lead counts matter more than any of them.
> - Read what happens to your accounts and content if you leave. Have a lawyer review the contract, because this guide is not legal advice.

## Start With What You Need Fixed

![Illustration of a checklist with one box marked in green beside a phone and a small storefront](/images/blog/how-to-choose-a-marketing-agency-local-business-1-needs.webp)

Write down three things before the first call:

1. **The result you want.** More phone calls, more quote requests, more walk-ins, or more work from a specific area. "More marketing" is not a goal.
2. **What you already have.** A Google Business Profile, a website, any ads running, past agencies and what happened with them.
3. **What you will not do.** Some owners will not sign a long commitment. Some need Spanish or Portuguese speaking customers reached in their own language. Some cannot hand over a large monthly ad budget. Say it early.

If you are not sure what an agency does day to day, read [what a marketing agency actually does for a local business](/en/blog/what-does-a-marketing-agency-do-local-business) first. It will help you tell a real service from a vague one.

## The Checklist

Use these eight checks in order. Each one is something you can verify, not just feel good about.

![Illustration of a ring of keys with one green key resting beside a closed folder](/images/blog/how-to-choose-a-marketing-agency-local-business-2-access.webp)

### 1. They explain the work in plain words

Ask: "What exactly will you do in the first stretch of work, and what will I see?" A good answer names tasks: fixing and completing your Business Profile, building or repairing pages, setting up call tracking, launching and managing a specific ad campaign. A weak answer is a list of buzzwords or the word "strategy" with nothing behind it.

### 2. Your accounts stay in your name

This check protects you more than any other. The accounts that carry your marketing, your Google Business Profile, your Google Ads account and your Search Console property, should belong to you, with the agency added as a user.

Google ties real control to ownership. It says you need to verify your business to edit your business info and interact with customers ([Google, "Verify your business", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7107242), accessed Oct. 2026). In Search Console, it says a verified owner has the highest degree of permissions ([Google, "Verify your site ownership", Search Console Help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9008080), accessed Oct. 2026). Whoever holds that role holds the keys.

Ask: "Who will be the owner on each account, and will I have full access from the start?" An agency that wants to be the sole owner is asking you to rent your own marketing from them. The rule is short: you own the accounts, they work inside them.

### 3. They do not promise a ranking

Be most skeptical of the confident promise: "We will get you to the top of Google." Google's own documentation contradicts it in two places.

For local results, Google says: "There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google" ([Google, "Improve your local ranking on Google", Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091), accessed Oct. 2026). For Search in general, it says it does not guarantee that it will crawl, index or serve your page ([Google Search Central, "In-depth guide to how Google Search works"](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/how-search-works), accessed Oct. 2026).

A good agency can tell you what it will work on, such as relevance, your profile, your reviews and your site, because Google says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and prominence. It cannot honestly sell you a position. The same goes for a promised number of leads or a promised date for results. Nobody controls those.

### 4. They separate their fee from your ad spend

If you run ads, two different costs exist: what you pay the agency to manage the campaigns, and what you pay Google for the clicks. Ask for both in writing, separately, and ask where the ad money goes.

Also be careful with a promised price per click. In Google Ads, the maximum bid is the most you tell Google you are willing to pay for a click, and Google says how much you actually pay is often less ([Google, "About Ad Rank", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722122), accessed Oct. 2026). The same page lists what feeds the auction: your bid, the quality of your ads and landing page, Ad Rank thresholds, how competitive the auction is, the context of the search, and the expected impact of ad assets. Cost varies with all of that, so a fixed price per click is a sales line, not a fact. For a plain walkthrough of how ad budgets work, see [how much paid ads cost a local business](/en/blog/how-much-paid-ads-cost-local-business-guide).

### 5. They show you reports you can read

Ask to see a sample report before you sign. Then check it against what Google actually measures. In the Search Console performance report, Google defines clicks as the number of times a user clicked your site from Google Search results, impressions as how many times your site appeared in Search results, and click-through rate as the click count divided by the impression count ([Google, "Performance report (Search results)", Search Console Help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/7576553), accessed Oct. 2026). Position is where it needs care: in the chart view it is the average position of the topmost result from your site, and in the table view it is the average for the specific URL or grouping in that row.

A report that only shows impressions and "reach" while skipping calls, form fills and booked jobs is measuring the agency's activity, not your business. Ask how they count a lead, how they separate real inquiries from spam and wrong numbers, and who can see the raw numbers. If you want to read the underlying data yourself, our guide to [Google Search Console for local business owners](/en/blog/google-search-console-for-local-business-owners) shows how. Google also says you do not have to sign up for Search Console to be included in search results ([Google, "About Search Console", Search Console Help](https://support.google.com/webmasters/answer/9128668), accessed Oct. 2026), so it is a window for you, not a requirement.

### 6. You can check their work independently

Do not rely on the agency's own case studies. Check for yourself:

- **Search for the agency.** Do they show up for what they sell? An agency that claims to win local visibility for clients should have a visible presence of its own, with real reviews and a complete profile.
- **Look up the advertiser.** Google keeps a searchable repository of advertisers and the ads they have served, and you can search it by advertiser or website name and filter by date and targeted location ([Google, "Ads transparency", Advertising Policies Help](https://support.google.com/adspolicy/answer/13733850), accessed Oct. 2026). Search for the agency, and for client businesses it names. Treat what you find as a starting point for questions, not as proof of results.
- **Call two references** the agency names, and ask what they would do differently.
- **Read how they handle criticism.** Look at their own negative reviews. Our guide on [how to respond to negative Google reviews](/en/blog/how-to-respond-to-negative-google-reviews) shows what a calm, useful reply looks like, so you can judge theirs.

### 7. The contract shows you the exit

A contract is where a friendly pitch meets reality. Read it for these points:

- How long the commitment is and how you can end it.
- What happens to your accounts, website files, ad data and content if you leave.
- Who owns what the agency builds for you.
- What is included in the fee and what costs extra.

We do not give a "normal" commitment length, because no source supports one. Judge it against your own situation. If something is unclear, ask for it in plain words, and have a lawyer review the contract before you sign. This is a checklist for your questions, not legal advice.

### 8. They understand your market and your customers

An agency can be competent and still wrong for you. Ask whether it has worked with businesses like yours in the kind of area you serve, and whether it can reach your actual customers. If your customers search or call in Spanish or Portuguese, ask who writes the ads, the pages and the review replies, and how they are tested. A translated page and a page written for a community are not the same thing. ## Questions to Ask a Marketing Agency Before You Sign

![Illustration of blank speech bubbles in a row, one outlined in green, beside a clipboard](/images/blog/how-to-choose-a-marketing-agency-local-business-3-questions.webp)

Use these in the call. The answers matter, and so does how directly they are given.

1. What will you do for my business, task by task, and what will I see?
2. Who will own my Google Business Profile, Google Ads account and Search Console property?
3. Will I have full admin access to every account from the start?
4. What is your fee, what is the ad budget, and where does the ad money go?
5. How do you measure success, and which numbers will I see?
6. Who actually does the work, and who is my contact?
7. What do you need from me, and what happens if I am slow to respond?
8. What will you not promise?
9. What happens to my accounts, content and data if I leave?
10. Can I speak with current clients, and can I see a sample report?
11. What have you advised a client not to do?

The last question is a test: an agency that has never talked a client out of something is probably only selling.

## Red Flags in One List

- A guaranteed ranking, guaranteed leads or a guaranteed date for results.
- The agency wants to own your accounts, or will not name the account owner.
- No way to see your own ad spend.
- Reports full of activity and empty of calls and inquiries.
- Pressure to sign today, or a contract that does not explain how to leave.
- One package for every business, whatever your problem.

SEO is a special case, and one paragraph is enough here. Search work is slow to judge and easy to sell vaguely, so apply every check above with extra care. ## How to Compare Two Proposals

![Illustration of two proposal folders on either side of a balance scale with one pan tinted green](/images/blog/how-to-choose-a-marketing-agency-local-business-4-compare.webp)

Do not compare on price alone. Put both proposals side by side and score each on the same questions:

| Question | Agency A | Agency B |
|---|---|---|
| Names specific tasks and deliverables | | |
| You own the accounts, with full access | | |
| Fee and ad spend listed separately | | |
| No promised ranking, leads or dates | | |
| Report with calls and inquiries, not only reach | | |
| Clear exit terms | | |
| Understands your market and language | | |

The cheaper proposal that fails three rows is not cheaper.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I choose a marketing agency for a local business?

Decide what result you need, then check each agency on the same points: what work it will do, who owns your accounts, whether it promises rankings, how it separates fees from ad spend, what its reports show, and how you can leave. Verify its claims independently.

### What questions should I ask a marketing agency before I sign?

Ask who owns your Google Business Profile, Google Ads account and Search Console property, what exactly they will do, which numbers you will see, what they will not promise, and what happens to your accounts and data if you leave.

### Can a marketing agency guarantee a first-page ranking on Google?

No. For local results, Google says there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. For Search in general, it says it does not guarantee that it will crawl, index or serve your page.

### Should I own my Google Ads account and Business Profile?

Yes. You should be the owner, with the agency added as a user. Google ties editing and customer interaction on a Business Profile to verification, and says a verified owner in Search Console has the highest degree of permissions.

### How can I check whether an agency is legitimate?

Search for the agency and its clients, look the advertiser up in Google's Ads Transparency Center, call references it names, and read how it handles its own reviews. Treat what you find as a starting point for questions.
