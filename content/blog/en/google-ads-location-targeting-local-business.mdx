You run a plumbing, cleaning or landscaping company, and you serve a specific part of town. Then a lead calls from a city you do not cover, and you wonder whether your ads are pointed at the wrong place. Location targeting is the Google Ads setting that answers that question, and it has one detail most owners never open: a choice about who counts as "in" your area.

This guide shows how to define your service area in Google Ads, how to choose between a radius and a named area, what the broad "Presence or Interest" setting means for your campaign, and how to check the choice before you pay for a wider footprint.

> **TL;DR**
> - Location targeting tells Google Ads where your ads can show. Define it from where you actually work, not from where you wish you worked.
> - Radius targeting needs a radius of at least 1 km around any given location, which is a little over half a mile ([Google, "Target ads to geographic locations", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722043), accessed Oct. 2026).
> - Google calls its broad setting "Presence or Interest." Check which setting your campaign uses before you assume ads reach only people inside your area.
> - Before widening your area, use Keyword Planner to see estimates for the new area. An estimate is not a guarantee.
> - Judge the setup by the calls and forms you receive from places you serve.

## What Location Targeting Controls

![A simple map of city blocks with a green circle drawn around a few blocks and a pin at the center](/images/blog/google-ads-location-targeting-local-business-1-circle.webp)

Every Google Ads campaign has a location setting. It is the first filter between your ad and a search. You tell Google where you want your ads to be eligible to show, which is exactly why this setting matters for a business with a limited service area.

Google's help page covers a radius around a location and, as a separate topic, broad geo targeting, which it names "Presence or Interest" ([Google, "Target ads to geographic locations", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722043), accessed Oct. 2026). So there are really two decisions, not one:

1. **The shape of your area.** A radius around a point, or a named area you pick from the location options.
2. **Who counts as in that area.** The "Presence or Interest" setting versus a narrower choice.

Most owners only make the first decision. The second one quietly decides who sees your ad.

## "Presence or Interest": Check This Setting First

![Two simple toggle switches side by side, one narrow and one wide, with a pin and a green circle](/images/blog/google-ads-location-targeting-local-business-2-setting.webp)

Google refers to its broad option as follows: broad geo targeting ("Presence or Interest") "can help you reach people who not only exist in your targeted locations" ([Google, "Target ads to geographic locations", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722043), accessed Oct. 2026). The sentence goes on, so read the full text on Google's page. The words "not only" are enough to tell you that this setting is broader than a plain "people in my area" rule.

For a company that drives to the customer, that deserves a second look. If you only repair roofs in a handful of towns, you need to know whether your campaign is using the broad setting before you decide the location list is doing the work you think it is.

Here is a practical way to handle it:

- **Open your campaign settings** and find the location options. Read how the targeting choice is labeled for your campaign.
- **Compare it to your business.** Do customers come to you, or do you go to them? A service-area business usually wants the area to match where the crew can drive.
- **Write down what you expect** (for example, "only people in these towns"), then check your leads against that expectation after a while.

This guide does not say which setting is always right: Google's page does not either, and the answer depends on how your business sells. The broad option exists and has a name, which is reason enough to check it.

## Radius or Named Area: Choosing the Shape

![A neat row of three stacked cards connected by green arrows, each with a map pin](/images/blog/google-ads-location-targeting-local-business-3-steps.webp)

Both shapes can work. The decision comes down to how your territory really looks on a map.

**Use a radius when your reach is distance-based.** If your crew treats a certain drive from the shop as the limit, a circle fits. Google's rule here is simple: "Radius targeting requires setting a radius of at least 1 km around any given location" ([Google, "Target ads to geographic locations", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722043), accessed Oct. 2026). So you cannot draw a smaller circle. For a business that covers a few dense blocks, the smallest circle may still be wider than your real territory.

**Use named areas when your reach follows borders.** Some businesses serve specific cities or neighborhoods because of licensing, traffic or crew location, not because of distance. A circle can pull in a town you do not serve and miss one you do. Picking the places by name makes the map match your business instead of geometry.

**Mix them when the territory is irregular.** A common pattern for a business with one core area and one or two satellite towns is a main area plus the extra places you choose on purpose.

Two questions help you pick: where did your last ten jobs happen (plot them, even on paper), and which towns would you turn down if they called today? Your Maps presence is a separate matter, covered in [service area business and Google Business Profile](/en/blog/service-area-business-google-business-profile).

## Setting It Up Step by Step

Here is the order that keeps you from guessing.

### Step 1: Define your real service area first

Before you touch the account, decide where you actually do work. Write the list of cities, neighborhoods or the distance your crew will travel. This is a business decision, not a Google Ads decision, and the campaign should copy it.

### Step 2: Pick the shape in the campaign settings

Go to the campaign's location settings. Choose a radius if your limit is distance, or named areas if your limit is borders. If you use a radius, remember the minimum of 1 km.

### Step 3: Check the targeting choice

Look at whether your campaign uses the broad "Presence or Interest" option. Decide on purpose, based on how your customers find you, instead of leaving the default in place by accident.

### Step 4: Keep the ad and the page consistent with the area

Say the city or area in the ad text and on the landing page. It helps a person in your area recognize that the ad is for them, and gives someone outside it a reason to move on. Our guide to [Google Ads Quality Score for local businesses](/en/blog/google-ads-quality-score-local-business) explains why the match between search, ad and page matters.

### Step 5: Filter out the wrong searches too

Location is one filter, search terms are another. Our guide on [negative keywords for local businesses](/en/blog/negative-keywords-google-ads-local-business) shows how to cut the wrong ones.

### Step 6: Review the leads, not just the clicks

When the calls and forms arrive, note where each person is located. If many come from places you do not serve, revisit Steps 2 and 3. If you are not sure how to handle the contacts once they arrive, read [what to do with leads from Google Ads](/en/blog/what-to-do-with-leads-from-google-ads).

## Before You Widen the Area, Estimate First

![A calculator and a magnifying glass over a small map with a green dot](/images/blog/google-ads-location-targeting-local-business-4-estimate.webp)

You may want to reach beyond the original map, because a new crew started or a neighboring town looks promising. Look at estimates before adding places.

Google's Keyword Planner is built for that kind of check. The help page says it "helps you research keywords for your Search campaigns," lets you "view the average cost for your ad to show on searches for a keyword," and shows a forecast of "how many conversions, clicks, or impressions you're likely to get for your keywords based on your spend" ([Google, "Use Keyword Planner", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/7337243), accessed Oct. 2026). To access basic features, you must complete your account setup by entering your billing information.

Two cautions keep the estimates honest:

- **The top-of-page bid estimate is not a guarantee.** Google defines it as "The bid you likely need to set for your ad to be shown among the ads at the top of the first page of search results" ([Google, "Top of page bid estimate: Definition", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6292661), accessed Oct. 2026), and says the estimate is not a guarantee.
- **Average CPC is a result, not a setting.** Google defines it as "calculated by dividing the total cost of your clicks by the total number of clicks" ([Google, "Average cost-per-click (Avg. CPC): Definition", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/14074), accessed Oct. 2026). It tells you what happened, not what a new area will cost.

Remember also that your bid is a ceiling. A click "won't cost you more than the maximum cost-per-click bid (or "max. CPC") that you set" ([Google, "About maximum CPC bidding", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/6326), accessed Oct. 2026), and Google notes that what you pay "is often less" than your maximum, because the auction weighs factors such as "the competitiveness of an auction" and "the context of the person's search" ([Google, "About Ad Rank", Google Ads Help](https://support.google.com/google-ads/answer/1722122), accessed Oct. 2026). A new area can come with different competition, so do not assume the numbers from your home area carry over. Pull the estimate for the new place, then decide. For the bigger picture on budgets, see [how much Google Ads cost a small business](/en/blog/how-much-google-ads-cost-small-business).

When you expand, change one thing at a time. Add the new area to one campaign, keep the old area as it was, and compare the leads from each. That way you can tell whether the new place earns its keep.

## Common Location Targeting Mistakes

- **Drawing the circle around where you wish you worked.** Start from the jobs you would accept today.
- **Ignoring the minimum radius.** If your territory is smaller than a circle of at least 1 km, named areas may fit better.
- **Never checking the targeting choice.** The "Presence or Interest" wording is easy to skip.
- **Judging by clicks.** Look at leads and where they come from.
- **Expanding everything at once.** Change one area at a time.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What is the smallest radius I can use in Google Ads?

Google states that radius targeting requires a radius of at least 1 km around any given location, which is a little over half a mile. If your real territory is smaller than that, consider named areas instead of a radius.

### What does "Presence or Interest" mean in Google Ads?

It is the name Google gives its broad geo targeting. Google's page says it can help you reach people who not only exist in your targeted locations. Check which setting your campaign uses so you know who your area includes, and compare your leads against what you expected.

### Should I target a radius or a city?

It depends on how your territory works. Use a radius when your limit is distance from your shop, and named areas when your limit follows borders such as city lines. Many businesses with one core area and a few extra towns mix the two.

### How do I know if I am reaching the wrong people?

Look at where your calls and form leads come from and compare that to the places you serve. If many come from outside your area, revisit the shape of your area and the targeting choice, and cut irrelevant searches with negative keywords.

## What to Take Away

- Location targeting is two decisions: the shape of your area and who counts as inside it.
- Radius targeting has a floor of at least 1 km, a little over half a mile.
- "Presence or Interest" is Google's name for broad geo targeting. Check it on purpose.
- Use Keyword Planner estimates before widening, and treat them as estimates, not promises.
- Review leads by location and change one area at a time.
