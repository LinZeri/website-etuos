Your profile is live, verified and showing up. You are just not in the top three. That is a different problem from a missing listing, and it needs a different kind of answer. Nothing is broken. The question is what pushes one business above another when both are eligible to appear.

This guide covers that question. It explains what Google says moves local results, which parts you can change, which parts nobody can change, and which popular shortcuts do real damage. If your profile does not show up at all, start with our guide on [why your business is not showing on Google Maps](/en/blog/why-my-business-not-showing-google-maps) and come back here afterward.

> **Key Takeaways**
> - Google says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and prominence, and that you cannot pay for a better local ranking.
> - You control relevance and a good part of prominence. You do not control distance, and no setting changes it.
> - Reviews help, but only honest ones: incentives and selective asking break Google's rules.
> - Shortcuts such as keywords in the business name or a virtual office address can get a profile suspended instead of promoted.
> - Nobody can promise you a position, and nobody can say how long a change takes to show.

## What Does Google Say Moves the Map?

![Illustration of three pillars of equal height holding up a single map pin with a green center](/images/blog/how-to-rank-higher-on-google-maps-1-fatores.webp)

Google answers this on its own help page, and the answer is short. Local results are "mainly based on relevance, distance, and popularity," and the page defines each factor ([Google, "Improve your local ranking on Google", Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091), accessed Oct. 2026).

- **Relevance** is "how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for."
- **Distance** "refers to how far each business is from the customer who's searching."
- **Prominence** "means how well-known a business is."

The same page adds that there is "no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google." That sentence rules out a whole category of offers. Anyone who sells a guaranteed spot in the map pack is selling something Google says does not exist.

Notice what the page does not give you: a weight for each factor. You will see articles with neat percentages for every ranking signal. We do not use them, because we have no verified source for them. Treat the three factors as a way to sort your to-do list, not as a formula.

## Relevance: Do You Match What the Searcher Typed?

![Illustration of a shop door with a clear signboard and three tags, one highlighted in green](/images/blog/how-to-rank-higher-on-google-maps-2-categoria.webp)

Relevance is the factor most under your control, and most of it comes down to describing your business accurately and then stopping.

**Pick the primary category with care.** Google's guidance is to "use as few categories as possible to describe your overall core business from the provided list" ([Google, "Guidelines for representing your business on Google", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177), accessed Oct. 2026). The primary category is your clearest statement of what you are. A florist who also sells gift baskets is still a florist. Choose the most specific option that describes the core of the business, add only secondary categories that are true, and leave it there.

**Keep the name honest.** The same guidelines say that "including unnecessary information in your business name isn't permitted." Your name should be the name on your sign, your invoices and your website. We come back to this in the shortcuts section, because it is the most common way owners hurt themselves.

**Write the description for people.** The profile description has a limit of 750 characters, cannot contain URLs or HTML, and should focus on your business rather than promotions, prices or sales ([Google, "Edit your Business Profile", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3039617), accessed Oct. 2026). Google's page does not say the description is a ranking input, so write it for the customer who reads it, not for an algorithm.

**Make the profile and the website say the same thing.** If your profile says you do emergency plumbing and your website never mentions it, the signal is weaker than it could be. Relevance is also something the searcher sees: someone comparing three profiles chooses the one that clearly offers what they need. For the full setup, see our [Google Business Profile checklist](/en/blog/google-business-profile-optimization-checklist). We do not repeat it here.

**Service areas describe where you work, not where you rank.** If you travel to customers, Google lets you set up to 20 service areas based on cities, postal codes or other areas you serve ([Google, "Manage your service areas for service-area & hybrid businesses", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9157481), accessed Oct. 2026). Listing an area is not a promise of showing up there. Our guide on [service-area businesses and Google Business Profile](/en/blog/service-area-business-google-business-profile) explains the setup.

## Distance: The Lever Nobody Controls

Distance is measured from the person searching, not from a spot you pick. That is why you can appear when you search from your shop and disappear when a customer searches from across town. Both results are normal, and [our troubleshooting guide](/en/blog/why-my-business-not-showing-google-maps) shows how to test it. The honest takeaway is short: you cannot move the searcher, so put your effort into the two factors you can work on.

## Prominence: Are You Well Known Enough to Be Trusted?

![Illustration of speech bubbles with stars being collected into a jar, one green star on top, and a hand holding a phone](/images/blog/how-to-rank-higher-on-google-maps-3-avaliacoes.webp)

Prominence is the broadest of the three. Google says it is "also based on info like how many websites link to your business and how many reviews you have," and that "more reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking" ([Google, Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091), accessed Oct. 2026).

That gives you two practical directions: reviews, and mentions of your business elsewhere on the web.

### Reviews

Reviews are the part of prominence an owner can influence every week. They also work on the customer, not only on the map. In BrightLocal's 2026 survey, 31% of US consumers said they will only use businesses rated 4.5 stars or higher, up from 17%, and 80% said they are more likely to use a business that replies to all of its reviews ([BrightLocal, 2026, via PinMeTo](https://www.pinmeto.com/news/brightlocal-local-consumer-review-survey-2026/), accessed Oct. 2026). That is stated preference in a survey, not observed behavior, and the data does not prove that replying raises your rank. It does show that a thin or neglected review section costs you at the moment of choice, even when the map put you in front of the customer.

What helps, in plain terms:

1. **Ask every customer, not only the happy ones.** Google's own guidance on asking for reviews explains how to create a review link or QR code, and it lets you share that link by email, WhatsApp message or a printed code ([Google, "Create a Google link or QR code to request reviews", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/16816815), accessed Oct. 2026).
2. **Make asking a routine,** not a campaign. A routine keeps your rating current and keeps you from forgetting to ask. Our guide on [getting Google reviews without awkward asking](/en/blog/how-to-get-google-reviews-for-local-business) has the scripts.
3. **Reply to the reviews you get.** Replying requires a verified business, and the person who wrote the review is notified when you reply ([Google, "Manage customer reviews", Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3474050), accessed Oct. 2026). Keep replies professional and short. For the hard ones, see how to [respond to negative Google reviews](/en/blog/how-to-respond-to-negative-google-reviews).

### Everything outside the profile

Because prominence includes how many websites link to you, your website and your presence elsewhere matter. A site with real pages for each service, mentions in local directories you actually belong to, and a name, address and phone number that match everywhere all give Google more evidence that you are a real, established business. We have no verified data on how much any one of these moves the map, so we will not rank them for you. Our [guide to local SEO for service businesses](/en/blog/local-seo-for-service-businesses-complete-guide) covers the website side.

Photos are the same story. They help customers decide, and you should add real ones, but we have no reliable source on how much they affect ranking, so we treat them as something the customer sees, not as a lever.

## What Do You Control and What Don't You?

Sorting your effort this way saves a lot of frustration.

| Factor | You control it? | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Relevance (category, name, description, services) | Yes | Describe the real business accurately and keep it consistent with your website |
| Prominence: reviews | Mostly | Ask every customer, reply to every review, never use incentives |
| Prominence: web presence | Partly | Build real service pages and keep your details identical across the web |
| Distance from the searcher | No | Accept it, and strengthen the other two factors |
| Paying for a better local ranking | Not possible | Google says there is no way to do it |

If you are choosing where to start, relevance is the cheapest lever, because it is a matter of accuracy and not of volume.

## Which Shortcuts Backfire?

![Illustration of a long winding road to a map pin with a shortcut blocked by a green barrier](/images/blog/how-to-rank-higher-on-google-maps-4-atalhos.webp)

When a profile stalls, owners and some agencies reach for tricks. These are the ones that tend to cost more than they gain.

**Keywords in the business name.** Adding "Best Cheap Plumber Miami" to a name that is just "Rivera Plumbing" looks like free relevance. Google's guidelines say that including unnecessary information in your business name isn't permitted, and they warn that doing so "could result in the suspension of your Business Profile" ([Google, "Guidelines for representing your business on Google", Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177), accessed Oct. 2026).

**A virtual office or mailbox address.** A prettier address in a better neighborhood feels like it would help with distance. The guidelines say a rented mailing address where you do not operate is a virtual office and "that location isn't eligible for a Business Profile," and that "P.O. boxes or mailboxes located at remote locations aren't acceptable." If you serve customers at their location, the right move is to hide your address, not to borrow another one.

**Paying or rewarding for reviews.** Google's content policy lists offering incentives, "such as payment, discounts, free goods and/or services," as a violation, and says fake engagement "will be removed" ([Google, Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7400114), accessed Oct. 2026). A discount for a five-star review may feel harmless. It is exactly the behavior the policy names.

**Asking only the customers you expect to be happy.** The same policy prohibits the practice of discouraging or prohibiting negative reviews, or selectively soliciting positive reviews from customers. The page says violations can lead to actions that range from suspending account privileges to account termination. A review profile built this way also reads as less trustworthy to customers.

**Service areas that stretch too far.** A long list of cities does not buy visibility in them. Google's guidelines say a service area should not extend farther than about 2 hours of driving time from where the business is based ([Google, "Guidelines for representing your business on Google", Google Business Profile Help](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177), accessed Oct. 2026). List the places you really serve.

**Buying "guaranteed ranking" packages.** Google says there is no way to pay for a better local ranking. Any package that promises a position is promising something outside its control.

The common thread: these tricks try to substitute for relevance and prominence instead of building them, and they put the whole profile at risk. A suspended profile loses all the work you did on the legitimate parts.

## Does AI Search Change Any of This?

More people now start their local search with an AI tool. In a March 2026 survey of 1,120 US adults, 47% said they had used an AI tool to find a local business in the past month ([Yext, 2026](https://www.yext.com/resources/consumer-search-behaviors-findings), accessed Oct. 2026). Yext sells local presence software, so read that number with that in mind. The same research found that 93% or more of the users who got an AI recommendation for a local business took at least one verification step before acting.

The practical reading: a recommendation, from the map or from an AI tool, still gets checked. People look at your reviews, your site and your details. The work that supports your map position is the same work that holds up when someone double-checks you. We do not claim that any of it makes an AI tool recommend you.

## What Should You Do First?

A simple routine, in this order:

1. Confirm the basics are clean: real address or hidden address, a phone number under your control, no duplicate. Our troubleshooting guide walks through that.
2. Check that your primary category and name are accurate and nothing extra is stuffed in.
3. Make the profile and website describe the same services in the same words.
4. Turn review requests into a habit and reply to every review.
5. Test from the neighborhoods you want to win, and accept that results change with the searcher's location.
6. Track calls and direction requests as your signal, not only the position you see.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What are the main factors that decide Google Maps ranking?

Google says local results are mainly based on relevance, distance and prominence. Relevance is how well your profile matches the search, distance is how far you are from the person searching, and prominence is how well known your business is. Google does not publish a weight for each factor.

### Can I pay Google to rank higher on Google Maps?

No. Google states that there is no way to request or pay for a better local ranking. Paid ads are a separate product and do not change the local ranking itself.

### Do more reviews make me rank higher?

Google says more reviews and positive ratings can help your business's local ranking. It does not say by how much. Ask every customer honestly, reply to reviews, and never offer a reward for one.

### Can I add keywords to my business name to rank higher?

No. Google's guidelines say that including unnecessary information in your business name isn't permitted and can lead to suspension. Use the name customers see on your sign and your invoices.

### Why does my ranking change depending on where I search from?

Because distance is measured from the person searching. You can rank well near your location and lower in other neighborhoods, and no setting changes that.
